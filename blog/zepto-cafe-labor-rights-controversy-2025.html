Case Study: Zepto Cafe Gig Worker Exploitation Allegations (2025)

78%
Backlash Threat Score

In March 2025, quick-commerce unicorn Zepto faced its most serious public relations crisis when leaked screenshots of delivery partner earnings and working conditions went viral on social media. The controversy centered on Zepto Cafe—the company's cloud kitchen vertical—where gig workers alleged systematic wage suppression, opaque penalty deductions, and exploitative shift requirements that left many earning below minimum wage despite working 12-14 hour days.

"When gig economy platforms prioritize hyper-growth and investor metrics over transparent earnings structures and worker welfare, even a single viral exposé can trigger coordinated labor action and lasting reputational damage."

1. The Viral Exposé and Worker Testimonials

The crisis began when a Mumbai-based Zepto Cafe delivery partner posted screenshots of his weekly earnings breakdown on X (formerly Twitter), showing that after deductions for alleged "late deliveries," "order cancellations," and "quality complaints," his net take-home was ₹4,200 for 72 hours of work—approximately ₹58 per hour, well below Mumbai's minimum wage of ₹375 per day for unskilled labor.

The post went viral within hours, with thousands of current and former Zepto workers sharing similar earnings statements and alleging that the company's algorithmic penalty system was designed to artificially suppress payouts. Workers claimed that many penalties were imposed without explanation or appeals process, and that the company's promised "₹30,000+ monthly earnings" advertised in recruitment materials were unattainable for the majority of delivery partners.

Investigative journalists amplified the story, publishing interviews with delivery partners who described forced unpaid waiting times between orders, denial of bathroom breaks during peak hours, and retaliation—including account suspensions—against workers who questioned management or refused unsafe delivery assignments during extreme weather.

2. Coordinated Protests and Union Mobilization

Within a week of the initial viral post, delivery partner collectives organized coordinated strikes in Mumbai, Bengaluru, and Delhi NCR. Hundreds of workers logged out of the Zepto app simultaneously during peak lunch and dinner hours, causing order delays and service disruptions that trended on social media as frustrated customers posted screenshots of 2+ hour delivery estimates.

Labor unions and gig worker advocacy organizations joined the protests, demanding transparent earnings structures, elimination of opaque algorithmic penalties, guaranteed minimum per-order payouts, accident insurance, and recognition of delivery partners as employees rather than independent contractors. The Indian Federation of App-based Transport Workers (IFAT) filed a formal complaint with the Ministry of Labour and Employment, requesting an investigation into Zepto's labor practices.

The protests garnered significant mainstream media coverage, with primetime news debates framing Zepto as emblematic of broader exploitation in India's gig economy. Opposition politicians seized on the controversy to criticize the government's failure to regulate platform labor, while some customer groups announced solidarity boycotts until worker demands were met.

3. Company Response and Damage Control Missteps

Zepto's initial response compounded the crisis. The company issued a statement claiming that the viral screenshots were "cherry-picked outliers" and that the majority of delivery partners earned above advertised benchmarks. However, the company refused to release aggregate earnings data or allow independent audits of its payout algorithms, fueling perceptions of opacity and defensiveness.

When asked about specific penalty cases, Zepto representatives stated that penalties were "necessary for quality control" and that workers who disagreed with deductions could raise tickets through the app's support system—a process workers had already documented as unresponsive, with complaints frequently auto-closed without resolution.

The tone-deaf response triggered a second wave of backlash. Tech commentators accused Zepto of treating workers as "disposable execution units" rather than essential partners in the company's $5 billion valuation. Investor scrutiny intensified, with reports emerging that some institutional backers had raised concerns during board meetings about ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) risks posed by Zepto's labor model.

Under mounting pressure, Zepto announced a "Workers First" initiative in late March 2025, committing to cap algorithmic penalties at 10% of weekly earnings, establish a dedicated worker grievance redressal cell, and provide accident insurance coverage. However, the company stopped short of reclassifying delivery partners as employees or disclosing full earnings transparency—key demands that unions and activists continued to press.

4. Long-Term Reputational and Operational Impact

Brand sentiment analysis showed a sharp decline in consumer trust, particularly among urban millennials and Gen Z users who expressed discomfort with supporting platforms accused of worker exploitation. While order volumes recovered partially after the strikes ended, Zepto's Net Promoter Score (NPS) dropped 18 points in affected markets, with qualitative surveys revealing that "ethical concerns" had become a top-three consideration for customers evaluating quick-commerce alternatives.

The controversy also created operational vulnerabilities. Delivery partner attrition spiked, forcing Zepto to increase sign-up bonuses and relax onboarding standards to maintain fleet capacity—measures that ironically increased per-order costs and strained the company's path to profitability. Competitor platforms, including Swiggy Instamart and Blinkit, capitalized on the crisis by publicly highlighting their own worker welfare initiatives in recruitment campaigns targeting Zepto's delivery fleet.

5. Strategic Takeaway

This case underscores the reputational and operational risks of gig economy labor models built on algorithmic opacity and adversarial worker relations. In an era of heightened labor consciousness and social media transparency, companies cannot rely solely on legal classification loopholes (independent contractors vs. employees) to shield themselves from public scrutiny over working conditions and earnings fairness.

Brands must proactively design transparent, defensible compensation structures and invest in genuine worker grievance mechanisms—not as crisis responses, but as foundational elements of platform design. When workers feel systematically exploited, they will organize, media will amplify, and consumer backlash will follow, regardless of whether the platform's practices are technically legal.

Additionally, companies should recognize that defensive, data-withholding responses to labor controversies almost always backfire. Transparency—publishing aggregate earnings data, subjecting algorithms to third-party audits, and engaging with worker representatives—builds credibility and often reveals that conditions are better than viral anecdotes suggest. When companies refuse transparency, stakeholders assume the worst.
