Case Study: Myntra Ramadan Collection Hindu Backlash (2025)

71%
Backlash Threat Score

In February 2025, Flipkart-owned fashion e-commerce platform Myntra launched a dedicated "Ramadan Edit" featuring curated ethnic wear, modest fashion, and festive gifting collections. While the campaign was designed to serve Muslim customers during the holy month, it triggered intense backlash from Hindu nationalist groups who accused Myntra of religious favoritism, alleging the platform aggressively marketed Islamic festivals while downplaying or ignoring Hindu celebrations.

"Festive marketing campaigns for minority religious communities, when perceived as more prominent than majority festival promotions, can trigger accusations of religious favoritism in politically charged environments—even when the actual marketing spend is proportional to customer demographics."

1. The Campaign Launch and Initial Backlash

Myntra's Ramadan Edit featured a homepage banner, dedicated landing page, influencer partnerships with Muslim fashion creators, and personalized push notifications to users who had previously browsed ethnic or modest fashion categories. The campaign highlighted abayas, kurtis, sherwanis, prayer mats, and gifting options, positioning Myntra as a one-stop destination for Ramadan shopping.

Within 24 hours, right-wing social media accounts began circulating screenshots of the campaign alongside accusations that Myntra was "pandering to Islamists" while neglecting Hindu festivals. Critics pointed out that Myntra's recent Makar Sankranti and Pongal campaigns had received far less prominent homepage placement and marketing budget compared to the Ramadan Edit, despite Hindus constituting over 80% of India's population.

Hashtags like #BoycottMyntra and #AntiHinduMyntra trended regionally, with thousands of users posting screenshots of uninstalling the app and pledging to shop with competitors. Some users alleged that Myntra was part of a broader corporate trend of "Hindu-phobia" where brands enthusiastically celebrate minority festivals for woke signaling but treat Hindu festivals as commercial afterthoughts.

2. Comparative Marketing Analysis and Perception Gap

Digital marketing analysts conducted side-by-side comparisons of Myntra's festive campaigns across different religious occasions. The analysis revealed that while Myntra had indeed run major campaigns for Diwali, Holi, and Navratri—with significantly higher ad spends than the Ramadan Edit—the visual prominence and influencer amplification of the Ramadan campaign created a perception of disproportionate attention.

The perception gap stemmed from several factors. First, the Ramadan campaign featured more culturally specific imagery and language (Arabic calligraphy, Islamic motifs) compared to Hindu festival campaigns, which often used generic "festive wear" branding that could apply to any celebration. Second, the campaign coincided with heightened political tensions around Muslim identity issues, making any Muslim-focused marketing more visible and subject to scrutiny.

Critics also pointed to a broader pattern across Indian e-commerce and advertising, where brands were increasingly careful about Hindu religious imagery—avoiding depictions of deities, sacred symbols, or ritual practices to prevent backlash—while feeling freer to market Islamic, Christian, or Sikh festivals with explicit religious iconography. This asymmetry, whether real or perceived, fueled allegations of corporate "appeasement" of minorities at Hindus' expense.

3. Company Response and Balancing Act

Myntra initially issued a statement emphasizing its commitment to celebrating "all festivals and communities" and pointing to its extensive Diwali, Navratri, and Holi campaigns as evidence of equal treatment. The company noted that festive collections were designed to serve diverse customer segments and that the Ramadan Edit represented a small fraction of overall marketing spend relative to Hindu festival campaigns.

However, the statement was met with skepticism. Critics argued that Myntra's response demonstrated the exact problem—brands treating Hindu festivals as commercial opportunities ("festive sales") while framing minority festivals as identity-affirming cultural celebrations ("Ramadan Edit," not "Eid Sale"). The linguistic and positioning differences, they contended, revealed an underlying bias in how brands approached different religious communities.

To contain the crisis, Myntra quietly adjusted its homepage to reduce the Ramadan Edit's banner prominence and launched a last-minute "Maha Shivratri Special" campaign with elevated visual placement—a move that satisfied neither camp. Muslim users felt the company was capitulating to majoritarian pressure, while Hindu nationalist accounts dismissed the Maha Shivratri campaign as a "damage control gimmick" rather than genuine respect for Hindu traditions.

4. Long-Term Brand Polarization Risks

Brand sentiment analysis revealed a polarization effect: while hardcore boycott calls came from a vocal minority, a larger segment of Hindu consumers reported increased sensitivity to perceived anti-Hindu bias in corporate marketing, making them more likely to scrutinize future campaigns and switch brands over cultural positioning issues.

Simultaneously, Myntra faced criticism from progressive consumers and Muslim users who viewed the company's response as caving to intolerant demands. Some Muslim fashion influencers terminated partnership agreements, stating they would not work with brands that treat inclusive marketing as a liability requiring apologies rather than a normal business practice in a diverse market.

5. Strategic Takeaway

This case illustrates the extreme difficulty of festive marketing in India's politically charged religious landscape. Brands face a no-win scenario: marketing to minority communities can trigger accusations of appeasement and favoritism from nationalist groups, while avoiding such marketing alienates minority customers and progressive consumers who expect inclusive representation.

Companies must recognize that festive marketing is evaluated not just on absolute spend or campaign frequency, but on relative visibility, cultural specificity, and symbolic messaging. A Ramadan campaign that would be uncontroversial in a politically neutral environment becomes a flashpoint when perceived as more "authentic" or "respectful" than Hindu festival campaigns, regardless of actual budget allocation.

Brands should consider symmetrical approaches: if they market minority festivals with explicit religious and cultural iconography, they should do the same for Hindu festivals rather than defaulting to generic "festive sale" language that strips Hindu celebrations of religious meaning. Alternatively, brands can adopt culturally neutral festive marketing across all communities—but must accept that this approach may satisfy no one in an environment where every group seeks affirmation, not just commercial acknowledgment.

Most importantly, brands must prepare for the reality that festive marketing decisions will be interpreted through political and identity lenses far beyond their commercial intent, and that maintaining neutrality or "equal treatment" may be functionally impossible when different stakeholders use fundamentally different metrics to evaluate fairness.
